Production & secretion of hydroxylated bovine collagen and
other polymeric proteins in Bacillus subtilis

Anargyros Alexiou1, Sheila I. Jensen1, Carsten Jers1, Mehdi
Abdollahi3, Ivan Mijakovic2, Lei Yang1

1Novo Nordisk Foundation Center for Biosustainability, Technical University of

Abstract

Collagen is a polymeric protein with unique functional properties, including high water-
binding capacity, gel-forming ability, and emulsifying potential, making it widely used in
the food and pharmaceutical industries. However, traditional animal-derived extraction
methods raise ethical, environmental, and safety concerns. Precision fermentation offers
a sustainable alternative, though major challenges remain in efficient secretion and
proline hydroxylation, which are essential for collagen's structural and functional
stability. In this study, we report the successful recombinant production and secretion of
bovine collagen, and other polymeric proteins in Bacillus subtilis. By constructing and
screening a library of 32 secretion tags, we identified several signal peptides that enabled
efficient extracellular secretion of codon-optimized collagen fragments. Additionally, by
extending the size of collagen constructs - from short bioactive sequences to full-length
tropocollagen - we revealed the size-dependent limitations of collagen production,
particularly in sequences containing the native C-terminal propeptide. To introduce
proline hydroxylation, we co-expressed prolyl-4 hydroxylases (P4Hs) from Bacillus
anthracis (BaP4H) and Giant Mimivirus (MimiP4H). Proteomic analysis indicated limited
hydroxylation by BaP4H, while MimiP4H exhibited promising results, with >30% of
collagen-derived peptides showing hydroxylation in intracellular fractions. We
confirmed by using circular dichroism, the formation of triple-helical structures for all
constructs, with a similar melting temperature of 29.9°C, regardless of hydroxylation
status. Furthermore, we extended the secretion platform to other structural proteins,
including resilin and elastin. To our knowledge, this study represents the first successful
production of hydroxylated collagen and resilin in B. subtilis, laying the groundwork for
sustainable production of protein-based biopolymers for applications in food, materials,
and biomedical industries.

Introduction

Collagens are the most abundant proteins in the animal kingdom, accounting for
approximately one-third of the total protein content. The collagen superfamily consists
of 28 members in vertebrates (I - XXVIII) and all share a common structural motif, the
triple-helix1-3. This supramolecular structure is composed of three polypeptide chains,
stabilized by hydrogen bonds and the presence of the uniquely modified amino acid

                                                       46
hydroxyproline (Hyp - O)4,5. Each chain is composed of repeating Gly-X-Y sequences,
where X and Y are often proline and hydroxyproline, respectively6,7. This arrangement
provides collagen with characteristic tensile strength and thermal stability6. As a
structural member of the extracellular matrix, it plays a crucial role in providing
mechanical strength and resilience to connective tissues, while also promoting cell
adhesion, differentiation, and growth8-11.

Beyond its physiological role, collagen and its derivative, gelatin, exhibit unique
properties, such as high water-holding capacity, gel-forming ability and emulsifying
potential12. These attributes make collagen a coveted protein for applications in the
pharmaceutical, cosmetic and food industries12-19. As of 2025, the global market for
collagen and gelatin was valued at over 10 billion USD, with strong growth driven by the
demand for novel functional foods and peptide health supplements20. However, the
escalating demand for collagen-based products could be limited by the insufficient
amount of current primary material sources, failing to meet the projected needs21.

Traditionally, collagen has been extracted from animal tissues and bones, primarily from
bovine and porcine origins. However, during the last few decades, there has been a
significant shift away from these resources12,22. Animal agriculture has long been a key
primary protein source for the global population. Nonetheless, it is now recognized as a
significant contributor to greenhouse gas emissions and land use, both of which are
central drivers of the current climate crisis23-25. Additionally, the consumption of bovine
or porcine-derived products is restricted due to religious or ethical constraints, and
isolating native collagen involves expensive extraction methods, batch-to-batch
variations and the risk of transmissible diseases26-28. Hence, there is an urgent need for
novel alternative sources of protein, including collagen29,30.

Microbial fermentation has emerged as a compelling strategy for producing recombinant
collagen and collagen-like proteins31,32. Unlike traditional methods, precision
fermentation allows the controlled production of safe, high purity, and reproducible
biomolecules using genetically engineered microorganisms33. Over the past decades,
recombinant collagen has been produced with varying degrees of success in a diverse set
of host organisms that include bacteria, yeast, insect cells, plant cells and transgenic
plants33,34. Most of the efforts have suffered from low titers while the usual intracellular

                                                       47
production of recombinant collagen significantly increases the operating costs, hindering
the economic viability of commercial-scale production32,34. Additionally, a critical barrier
remains the inefficient post-translational modification of proline to hydroxyproline, a
reaction that is catalyzed by prolyl-4 hydroxylase (P4H) which is essential for achieving
the stable, functional structure of native collagen stable35. Expression of recombinant
animal P4Hs demonstrated low levels of solubility or enzymatic activity. However,
evidence suggests the presence of P4Hs in bacteria, algae, and viruses, could exhibit
similar functions as animal P4Hs36,37. Specifically, Bacillus anthracis P4H (BaP4H) and
Giant Virus Mimivirus P4H show higher hydroxylation efficiency when compared to their
animal counterparts when expressed in E. coli38-41. However, it is uncertain whether
similar activity can be achieved in other bacterial hosts.

Among bacterial platforms, Bacillus subtilis stands out as a promising chassis for
recombinant protein production due to its GRAS (generally regarded as safe) status, high
protein secretion capacity, and robust genetic tools42,43. Unlike hosts with limited
secretion capabilities, like E. coli, B. subtilis naturally secretes proteins into the
extracellular medium, simplifying downstream processing and enhancing cost-
effectiveness. These traits make it an ideal host to produce complex proteins, like
collagen. However, the efficiency of protein secretion is dependent on the interplay
between the secretion tag and the protein of interest. So far, no accurate prediction tools
have been developed, making the experimental exploration of the appropriate tag
essential44.

In this study, we present the first successful production and secretion of hydroxylated
recombinant collagen in Bacillus subtilis. To identify the best secretion signals for
collagen, we integrated a library of secretion peptides into the genome of an engineered
B. subtilis strain and conducted a systematic secretion assay. Based on these results, we
developed integrative and expression vectors enabling the production and secretion of
higher molecular-weight polymeric and collagen proteins. Additionally, we explored the
hydroxylation effects of two P4Hs on the recombinant collagen and assessed its impact
on the biophysical properties of collagen, such as triple-helix formation, which proved to
be stable at 29.9°C regardless of the hydroxylation status. Our findings establish the
foundation for an animal-free collagen production, paving the way for future applications
in food technology.

Results and discussion

    1. Efficient production and secretion of non-hydroxylated bovine collagen
    Although Bacillus subtilis has been widely used in industry for the secretion of
heterologous proteins and enzymes, the efficiency of secretion is dependent on the
interplay between the secretion tag (SPs) and protein sequence of interest (POI).

    To establish an efficient secretion chassis for recombinant bovine collagen, a 110
amino acid sequence of bovine collagen type I alpha I (10kDa) was selected, codon-
optimized and cloned with a library of 32 N-terminal secretion tags. The secretion tag
library was constructed by amplifying native Bacillus subtilis sequences, while the
collagen fragment was carefully chosen to (a) reflect the amino acid composition of full-
length collagen (Supplementary Table 1), (b) originate from a region predicted to form
a stable triple-helix, and (c) include putative bioactive motifs such as VPGDL and the
integrin 21-binding sequence DGEA45-48 . The 10kDa collagen was named BsbColv2.

    To achieve comparable results, integrative plasmids targeting the glmS locus of the
Bacillus subtilis genome were employed. Expression of collagen constructs was driven by
the strong constitutive promoter P3P49.

    2. Expanded pool of secreted collagen 1[I] sequences and polymeric proteins
    To explore if the selected secretion tags can translate similar results to polymeric
proteins of greater molecular weight, we decided to evaluate larger sequences stemming
from bovine collagen type I, including full-length collagen and procollagen. Additionally,

we expanded the application of the strain to production of other protein-based
biopolymers, such as elastin and resilin.

that the 20kDa collagen was successfully produced and secreted by all the secretion tags,

with minor fluctuations in secretion efficiency. When compared to the previously

produced 10kDa fragment (PC), the novel collagen shows similar or minor decreased

for the 450 amino acid construct was limited only in the intracellular fraction and in

that the extensive resources required by expanded collagens, considerably hinder and
stress B. subtilis production capabilities, is a size dependent manner.

The C-propeptide affects the secretion of 1[I] and 2[I] collagen chains
         Furthermore, we focused on other regions that control the assembly of collagen.

In nature, collagen type I is composed of a 2:1 ratio of alpha 1 (a1[I]) and alpha 2 (a2[I])

53
chains. The C-terminal propeptide, also known as the C-propeptide, plays a crucial role in
collagen assembly and fibril formation both inside and outside cells. It guides the
selection and association of collagen alpha chains, ensuring proper folding and
preventing premature fibril formation within cells. Once the procollagen molecule is
secreted, the C-propeptide is cleaved by specific native proteinases, leading to the release
of the mature collagen molecule and subsequent fibril assembly. To investigate the
manifestation of similar effects, we cloned the codon-optimized C-terminal sequences of
both alpha I and alpha II chains with and without their respective C-propeptides. We
found out that the secretion of alpha I chain with the C-propeptide was possible for three
out of the four secretion tags, with peptide SP26 being the most efficient (Figure 7C). On
the contrary, the alpha 2 chain sequence that maintains the C-propeptide, was not able to
be secreted (Figure 7C). Further experiments showed that limited amounts of alpha 2
collagen are produced and retained inside the bacterial cells (Supplementary Figure 2).
Notably, the secretion of alpha 1 and alpha 2 sequences lacking their respective C-
propeptides yields different results. Both chains are able to be secreted in the production
media in relatively similar amounts (Figure 7D). Particularly, the consistency of
secretion irrespectively of the presence of the C-propeptide or not, renders secretion tag
SP26 an ideal partner for production of the 1[I] chain. Although the two chains are
designed to have similar molecular weight, the electrophoretic mobility of 2 chain is
higher than that of 1, a phenomenon that aligns with that of native 1[I] and 2[I]
chains51. Interestingly, a single degradation band is visible for both chains, indicating a
precise degradation pattern, possibly by an unknown host-specific protease. When
compared against the previous 10kDa collagen, the production was visibly significantly
lower.

Successful production of full-length tropocollagen from bovine cDNA
    As a next step, we explored the production of procollagen (138kDa) and tropocollagen

type I alpha I (96kD) - full length collagens with and without their N- and C- terminal
propeptides, respectively. The polymeric and high in GC% collagen sequence does not
allow the production of long sequences from DNA synthesis companies, even with
extensive codon-optimization. For this reason, we decided to amplify the bovine
procollagen and collagen 1[I] sequences directly from purchased bovine skin cDNA. As
with previous constructs, we integrated the genes and their respective secretion tags to

                                                       54
the glmS locus of Bacillus subtilis KO7S strain and tested their production. Interestingly,
it was revealed that the production and secretion of tropocollagen is possible in B. subtilis
for two out of four secretion peptides - SP5 and SP19, albeit in smaller amounts (Figure
7F) However, this is an important milestone, as the number of successfully secreted
proteins >90 kDa is limited in academic literature. By increasing the total volume loaded
in SDS-PAGE, we found out that the production of tropocollagen is possible from the other
secretion tags as well, but with a significant loss in titers (Figure 7G). Furthermore, it
was apparent that thaw-freeze cycles or proteolytic activity produced degradation
products (Figure 7G). On the contrary, procollagen was not produced in either
intracellular or extracellular fraction, demonstrating that potentially sequences in N- or
C-terminal propeptides hinder collagen production. Nevertheless, the expression and
production of a full-length collagen from a mammalian cDNA remains remarkable as the
sequence is not codon-optimized and Bacillus subtilis 168 does not exhibit high codon
usage for codons like CCC which is prominent in collagen sequence and translates to
proline.

    To investigate if codon-optimized versions could prove advantageous for the
production of procollagens 1[I] and 2[I], we assembled synthetic constructs by USER
cloning codon-optimized segments to approximate the full-length sequences, while
maintaining the native amino acid content. These constructs were integrated into the
glmS locus and paired with the validated SP5 secretion tag as before. Western blotting
showed that procollagen was produced in small amounts, but it was maintained inside
the cell (Supplementary Figure 3). Future experiments will aim on testing more signal
peptides to achieve secretion and compare the production of codon-optimized
tropocollagen to the cDNA-derived tropocollagen.

Achieving production and secretion of other protein-based biopolymers: Elastin &
Resilin

    To assess the ability of Bacillus subtilis 168 to produce other biopolymers, we decided
to explore the production of other codon-optimized elastomeric proteins such as elastin
and resilin. Similarly to collagen, these proteins are characterized by repeated, glycine-
and proline-rich domains that confer elastic and self-assembly properties. Resilin, for
example, is purported to be one of the most resilient elastic materials known52.
Additionally, elastin often co-localizes with collagen in the extracellular matrix and 20-
24% of its prolines are hydroxylated53. These shared structural and biochemical features
make elastin a promising candidate for transferring insights we are gaining from the
development of our collagen production platform and open the possibility of co-utilizing
our biopolymers in future applications.

     For this study, we employed the pHT100 plasmid that contains the IPTG-inducible
pgrac100 promoter. pHT100 is a mid-copy plasmid and it has been described as a strong
driver of protein production54. In a similar manner as described before, we assembled the
biopolymers' sequence by USER cloning codon-optimized segments together (Figure 8A).
SP5 was used again as secretion tag. However, the His tag was placed in the N-terminal
directly after the secretion tag followed by a TEV cleavage site. After protein production
experiments in Cal18-2, we tested the detection of elastin or resilin in either intracellular
or secreted fractions. Western blots revealed the efficient secretion of both resilin and
elastin in the production media, with limited amounts maintained in the cell lysates
(Figure 8). Notably, the secretion of elastin, proved to be inconsistent between batches,
with some experiments revealing the absence of elastin secretion (Supplementary
Figure 3). On the contrary, resilin secretion proved to be more robust leading to
successful purification in a larger batch. Interestingly, during dialysis on TBS at 4°C, the
resilin solution turned opaque (Supplementary Figure 4B). This phenomenon is
characteristic of native resilin as the protein's conformation is pH- and temperature-
dependent and exhibits an upper critical solution temperature (UCST) around 6°C
(Supplementary Figure 4A)55. Temperatures below the UCST, promote conformational
changes that lead to phase separation and visible opaqueness. This suggests that our
recombinant variant might mimic the native resilin's structure. Further experiments are
required to characterize the recombinant resilin and explore its potential applications.

Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that resilin has been
produced and secreted by a Bacillus subtilis strain.

    3. Hydroxylation of recombinant collagen by non-animal prolyl-4 hydroxylases

    Bacillus anthracis P4H exhibits low levels of expression and hydroxylation activity

    The post-translational modification of proline to hydroxyproline by prolyl-4
hydroxylase is crucial for the stability of collagen. To achieve collagen hydroxylation in
Bacillus subtilis, we employed B. anthracis P4H (BaP4H), known to act on collagen-like
sequences, and evaluated the enzymes activity on recombinant collagens in vivo. We
constructed two different systems for the co-expression of collagen and BaP4H. The first
one builds on the previously developed strains that constitutively express the 10kDa and
20kDa collagen genes coupled with SP5 signal peptide. These strains were transformed
by natural competence with a ProUSER plasmid, that bears homology arms for the rsbP
locus and the BaP4H codon-optimized gene, controlled by the Tet-inducible promoter. To
monitor BaP4H production, we added a N-terminal Strep-tag II. The enzyme has a
dimerization domain close to the N-terminal and an active site in the C-terminal.
Acknowledging that the Strep-tag might be affected by this conformation, we decided to
develop an additional construct and including a GS linker (spacer) between the Strep-tag
and the rest of the P4H sequence (Figure 9A). The second system represents an operon
system of BaP4H and the respective collagen (Figure 9B). For this, we used the pHT01

                                                       58
plasmid that includes the IPTG-inducible pgrac01 promoter. Western blot analysis
verified the production of collagen from both systems. It indicated that the production of
collagen from the operon system is more consistent when induced by 0.5mM IPTG
(Supplementary Figure 5). Additionally, the detection of Strep-tagged BaP4H was not
possible, however, Ponceau S staining provided indications of BaP4H presence inside the
cells (presence of distinct bands - Supplementary Figure 6).

    To investigate if BaP4H was produced and active, we performed proteomic analysis
on the cell pellets and on selected purified collagens (secreted fractions). For comparison,

included in this thesis). Unexpectedly, the BaP4H was detected only in samples of the
operon system. Compared to E. coli, which achieved BaP4H expression levels exceeding
6.2 × 1011 intensity units, B. subtilis exhibited significantly lower expression, reaching
only around 3 × 106 units--representing a difference of over five orders of magnitude
(Figure 10A). Of the purified secreted collagens, detectable hydroxylated peptides were
present in only 3 samples and include only 1 hydroxylated site per peptide (Figure 10b).
These samples include: the Operon-BaP4H-Collagen110, the glmS::Collagen110
rsbP::NoLinkerBaP4H and the glmS::Collagen225 rsbP::LinkerBaP4H. Although the

respectively, the absence or limited detection of hydroxylated peptides indicates that
hydroxylated fraction may be obscured by the non-hydroxylated counterparts indicating
that the occupancy rate of hydroxylated prolines is generally low. Thus, we conclude that
neither system as it stands, can facilitate efficient production of hydroxylated collagen.
We hypothesized that the low hydroxylation levels are a function of the low abundance
of BaP4H and the limited availability of cofactors. Most prolyl-4 hydroxylases need
oxygen, a-ketoglutarate, iron and ascorbate to fully function. For this reason, we decided
to increase the mRNA levels of BaP4H, supplement the media with ascorbate and explore
the use of an additional non-animal P4H.

    Optimization of P4Hs expression and hydroxylation conditions

         To optimize P4H and collagen expression, we focused on the operon system,
producing only Bacola225 (20 kDa collagen). Initially, we employed the pHT100 plasmid
to increase the mRNA levels through the pgrac100 IPTG-inducible promoter. Moreover,
we increased the number of P4H tested, by including Mimivirus P4H (MimiP4H) in our
analysis. This viral P4H has been proven to effectively work on collagen expressed in
prokaryotes, such as E. coli.

         To monitor the enzymes production, we substituted the Strep-tag II in the N-
terminal, with the Nano-Glo® HiBiT-tag, a constituent of a sensitive luciferase system for
protein detection. Expression of P4Hs - without collagen - in B. subtilis KO7S strains, and
subsequent Hibit-blotting experiments, revealed successful production and detection of

                                                       60
both enzymes (Figure 11A). However, the levels of Mimivirus P4H were significantly
lower than that of the BaP4H, suggesting the need for increased translation.

         The ribosomal binding site (RBS) is a significant factor controlling the production
levels of proteins. Hence, we decided to explore and optimize the strength of our RBSs
through the employment of RBS and Operon Calculator bioinformatic tools56. New
designs increased considerably the estimated P4H translation rate from 289au to
241386au and the collagen translation rate from 102358 to 197692 (Supplementary
Figure 7). To provide experimental validation, we aimed to construct plasmids bearing
either new or previous RBSs for both P4H and the 20kDa recombinant collagen, with or
without secretion tags. A significant increase in P4H or collagen titres would validate the
predicted results. The new RBSs were namely RBS3* for P4H and RBS4* for collagen,
instead of RBS1 and RBS2 as previously. The asterisks nominate their synthetic nature.

         Constructs expressing the RBS1 and RBS2 for BaP4H and MimiP4H were
successfully assembled. However, multiple cloning rounds failed to produce BaP4H-
NewRBS constructs without disruptive mutations on the BaP4H gene. It is known that
pgrac100 can be leaky in E. coli54, and elevated BaP4H levels with collagen could be
disruptive. Nevertheless, we managed to create operons for MimiP4H, that enable either
intracellular or extracellular production of the 20kD collagen, and we set to explore the
effect of RBS3* and RBS4* versus RBS1 and RBS2.

    4.2 LC-MS/MS revealed significant MimiP4H activity and low hydroxylation in secreted
         collagen

    A key challenge in microbial collagen production is achieving efficient proline
hydroxylation. Provided that hydroxylation occurs intracellularly before secretion, we

                                                       65
hypothesized that collagen localized inside the cells could exhibit higher hydroxylation
levels compared to secreted collagen molecules. This assumption is based on the idea that
secretion, especially through the Sec pathway, may limit the exposure time of collagen to
prolyl-4-hydroxylases (P4Hs), resulting in incomplete modification before extracellular
release. Additionally, hydroxylation may hinder secretion capacity. To explore these
hypotheses and verify the P4H activity of the enzymes, we decided to detect and compare
hydroxylation levels in intracellular versus secreted collagen fractions by LC-MS/MS,
expecting greater hydroxylation occupancy in intracellular pools due to prolonged
enzyme interaction.

    Mass spectrometry results demonstrated hydroxylation by both prolyl-4
hydroxylases and verified our hypothesis, by revealing significant elevated hydroxylation
in intracellular fractions, particularly when MimiP4H was co-expressed (Figure 14).
Notably, in SDA112 strain, more than 30% of the total intracellular peptides contained at
least one (>1) hydroxylated proline, significantly more than the 2.8% exhibited by BaP4H
(SDA108 - Figure 14C). Furthermore, SDA112 produced extensive proline
hydroxylation events, with specific peptides exhibiting up to 10 hydroxyprolines
simultaneously in the same peptide. This was the maximum possible number of
hydroxyprolines based on the maximum available prolines of the digested peptides. This,
in combination with the position of hydroxyprolines in the collagen sequence, suggests
that MimiP4H is hydroxylating prolines irrespectively of their X or Y position on the Gly-
X-Y motif (Figure 14B). Although high hydroxylation activity is desirable, uncontrolled
and excessive hydroxylation can invoke negative effects in triple-helix stability. On the
contrary, the maximum number of simultaneous hydroxyprolines detected on the BaP4H
strain (SDA108) was five, while 96% of the intracellular or secreted hydroxylated
peptides contained only 1 hydroxyproline, suggesting significantly low activity.

    Interestingly, even though the secreted proteins showed reduced hydroxylation
levels from 30% to 6.5%, SDA112-MimiP4H maintained similar distribution of the
hydroxylated peptides, irrespectively of the fraction (Figures 14E,F). Unexpectedly, the
intracellular production of collagen (SDA130) despite the presence of the highly efficient
MimiP4H, produced hydroxylation levels closer to BaP4H (SDA108) than that of SDA112,
reaching 4.4% of detected hydroxylated peptides (Figure 14C). SDA130 produced a
lower coverage level (70%) of the sequence compared to the rest of the samples ( 86%
coverage). This could mean that SDA130 had lower detection events, and the

                                                       66
hydroxylation levels were underestimated. Nevertheless, these results, in combination
with the low production titres, constitute SDA130 an inefficient producer of collagen.

    In conclusion, proteomics results revealed that extracellular fractions possess
significantly less hydroxylation than the intracellular fractions. MimiP4H shows
promising activity, but potentially exhibits uncontrolled hydroxylation, diverging from
the native hydroxylation profile of collagen (Figure 14B). BaP4H exhibits significantly
low activity despite high production levels (Figure 11A). SDA130 exhibited reduced
MimiP4H activity compared to SDA112, failing to show advantages over the other strains.

    4.3 Successful triple-helix conformation and thermal stability for secreted rCollagen
    To investigate the formation of triple-helical structures and the thermal stability of
recombinant collagens, we performed circular dichroism (CD) spectrometry at 4°C.
Triple-helical collagen presents characteristic CD spectra, with a negative peak near and
below 200nm and a positive peak near 221nm. By testing different dilutions of bovine
collagen type I, a universal concentration of 0.2mg/ml was selected as the most
consistent for subsequent experiments (Figure 15A). Non-hydroxylated collagen CD
(SDA91) produced a negative peak below 200nm but was lacking a distinct positive peak
near 221nm. This phenotype corresponds to the formation of a random coil or partially
assembled triple-helix. Experiments with higher concentrations of non-hydroxylated
collagen, produced CD spectra with a positive peak at 221.5 nm, revealing the formation
of a triple-helix (Supplementary Figure 12). Furthermore, purified secreted samples
from either BaP4H or MimiP4H formed triple-helical structures, with no distinct
differences between them or from the bovine standard (Figure 15A-D). Notably, the CD
spectra of the intracellular fractions suggested the formation of -helix instead of triple-
helix (Figure 16A-C). Intracellular fractions of the recombinant collagen still maintain
the sequence of the secretion tag 5, which forms an alpha helix (Supplementary Figure
13). Therefore, a possible explanation could be that the secretion tag affects the detection
of the triple-helical CD spectra or sterically hinders the assembly of the triple-helix.
Another hypothesis includes the presence of co-purified -helical proteins. Lampfish and
starfish collagen type I were also investigated and showed only a negative peak at 200nm,
suggesting the denaturation of collagen and random coil formation (Supplementary
Figure 12).
    To estimate the thermal stability of collagen samples, we conducted temperature-
dependent scans at 221.5nm and measured the changes in the ellipticity. The calculated
denaturation temperature (Td) of bovine collagen was at 39.9°C, which aligns to what is
documented in literature. Surprisingly, the Td of all the secreted recombinant collagens,
was estimated to be around 29.9°C with minor deviations.
     In conclusion, these findings, combined with the mass spectrometry results, indicate
that the hydroxylation of the secreted collagens is absent or limited for the BaP4H
(SDA108) and MimiP4H (SDA112) strains, respectively. As a result, the thermal stability
of the collagens remains similar to that of the non-hydroxylated collagen. Nevertheless,

                                                       68
the formation of triple-helical structures is possible and maintains a thermal stability of
almost 30°C. Although this temperature is lower than native bovine collagen (40°C), it is
comparable to or higher than that of the marine collagens (25-30°C). This suggests that
the recombinant collagens may be suitable for biotechnological applications, particularly
those where milder thermal stability is sufficient or even advantageous

 Conclusion
 In this study, we successfully demonstrated the first production and secretion of
 hydroxylated (bovine) collagen in Bacillus subtilis. By systematically screening 32
 secretion peptides, we identified the best secretion peptides that enhance extracellular
 collagen secretion, particularly peptides SP5, SP19, SP26, and SP3. By progressively
 increasing the length of produced collagen, we highlighted the size-dependent challenges
 in collagen secretion and indicated that sequences containing the C-terminal propeptide
 may hinder production.

 To introduce hydroxyproline in recombinant collagen, we co-expressed Bacillus anthracis
 P4H and Mimivirus P4H. Mass spectrometry revealed limited activity of Bacillus anthracis
 P4H, but relatively elevated activity in the intracellular fraction for Mimivirus P4H, with

                                                        70
>30% of collagen-derived peptides there showing relatively high levels of proline
hydroxylation. Additionally, MimiP4H showed unconditional hydroxylation of prolines
regardless of their position in the collagen sequence. Circular dichroism analysis revealed
the formation of triple-helix for all secreted recombinant collagen despite their
hydroxylation status, and their general stability up to 30°C. Interestingly, we showed that
intracellular collagens did not form triple helices. Beyond collagen, we extended our
secretion platform to other biopolymers, successfully producing the elastomers, resilin
and elastin.
Overall, this work establishes a promising production system for hydroxylated collagen
and opens the way for future food applications. Further research should focus on
enhancing hydroxylation efficiency of P4Hs, scaling production and increasing collagen
secretion titres.

    1. Bacterial strains, Media, and Growth conditions.
    For plasmid preparation, USER cloning and selection, E. coli Dh5a or TOP10 stains
(Thermo Fisher Scientific) were routinely grown in lysogeny broth (LB) media liquid cultures
under shaking at 225-250 rpm or on LB agar plates at 37°C according to standard protocols.
For protein production experiments, the extracellular protease-deficient B. subtilis KO7S
strains were grown in either Terrific broth or in Cal18-2 media61 (40 g/L yeast extract, 1.3 g/
MgSO4·7H2O, 50 g/L, maltodextrin (DE 13-17) Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA)), 20 g/L
NaH2PO4·2H2O, 6.7 mL/L Na2MoO4 stock solution (2.0 g/L), 6.7 mL/L trace metal solution
(consisting of 4.48 g/L MnSO4·H2O, 3.33 g/L FeCl3·6H2O, 0.625 g/L CuSO4·5H2O, and 7.12 g/L
ZnSO4·7H2O), and 100 L/L Pluronic L-61 (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA), then
adjusted to pH = 6). When appropriate, the media were supplemented with antibiotics
(100µg/mL ampicillin (Amp) or 50µg/mL kanamycin (Kan) for E. coli and 5µg/ml
chloramphenicol, 5µg/ml kanamycin and 10µg/ml erythromycin for B. subtilis KO7S).

    2. DNA manipulation, Plasmid and Strain Construction
    All primers employed in this study were ordered from Integrated DNA Technologies
(IDT) and are listed in Supplementary Table. The synthetic genes and fragments were
purchased from either Integrated DNA Technologies or Twist Bioscience and always codon-
optimized by the IDT codon-optimization tool. All plasmids and protein sequences
infromation is found in the Supplementary tables.
    For the construction of the B. subtilis collagen-secretion strains, the ProUSER2.0
integrative vectors were employed. The secretion peptides were native to Bacillus subtilis and
were PCR amplified from the genome of the wild-type strain Bacillus subtilis 168. The codon-
optimized gene of bovine collagen type I alpha I was synthesized according to the preferred
codon usage of B. subtilis (IDT DNA) and cloned to integrative vectors with their respective
secretion peptide partners. Specifically, 1µg of the ProUSER plasmid, pProUSER13C1B, that
bears homology arms for the B. subtilis glmS genomic locus, was digested by mixing with 1µl
of AsiSI (New England BioLabs, USA), 5µl CutSmart buffer and DNAse-free water to a final
volume of 50µl. The reaction was performed at 37°C for 16 hours followed by inactivation at
80°C for 20 minutes. In the same reaction, 1µl of the Nt.BbvCI was added, and the mix was

                                                       72
incubated for 1 hour at 37°C followed by inactivation at 80°C for 20 minutes. The appropriate
band that corresponds to the digested plasmid was purified by gel extraction (Macherey-
Nagel) after gel electrophoresis of 1 hour at 100V. Then, 10- 20ng of the nicked plasmid were
mixed with the appropriate PCR-amplified secretion tags and collagen protein (Phusion U)
and subsequently USER cloning protocol was followed as described before.

    For the episomal expression of the collagenous and P4Hs genes, plasmids pHT01 and
pHT100 were selected. Codon-optimized genes for B. anthracis P4H and Mimivirus P4H,
collagens and other polymeric genes, were synthesized and USER cloned after both vectors
and genes were amplified with PCR (Phusion U). For most P4H constructs, a Hibit-tag was
positioned the N-terminal for simultaneous detection of collagen and P4H expression. For the
Hibit-tag addition, complementary oligos were synthesized. Larger and highly repetitive

    4. Protein production in. B. subtilis

    Preliminary small-scale expression experiments and comparison of signal peptides
    Single colonies were picked and inoculated, depending on their number, in either 15ml
preculture tubes containing 5mL LB, or in 24 deep-well plates containing 3mL LB,
supplemented with the appropriate antibiotics. The cultures were grown O/N at 37°C and
225 rpm. The next day, the cultures were back-diluted to an OD of 0.1 in a new 24-deep well
with 3-4 mL of either fresh TB or Cal18-2 media. Strains containing plasmids were
supplemented with the appropriate antibiotics. Depending on the experimental purposes, the
cultures were grown at different times and temperatures, while maintain a steady 225rpm
shaking. For initial expression tests, as well as for the comparison of the secretion peptides,

                                                       73
a temperature of 20°C for 72 hours was selected whereas for the co-expression of collagens
and P4Hs, 30°C for 24 hours was preferred.

    Larger-scale protein production experiments
    Protein purifications were performed in large flasks of 2L containing 0.5L of terrific broth,
supplemented with 1mM of ascorbic acid, 0.01% anti-foam (Thermofischer) and 5-10 µg/mL
of the appropriate antibiotic. The media was inoculated at OD600 0.1, and the cultures were
grown at 37°C and 225rpm for 2hours for constitutive expression or until OD600 reached 0.6-
0.9 for induction with 1mM of IPTG. Then, the temperature was reduced to 30°C and the
cultivation was stopped 24hours after inoculation. The supernatant was separated from cell
debris by centrifugation at 7000g for 30 minutes at 4°C. Subsequently, the cell pellet was
resuspended in PBS, transferred to 50ml falcon tubes, and precipitated again with
centrifugation for the next steps of protein purification. Simultaneously, the supernatant was
passed through a 0.22µm filter and then concentrated with an Amicon Stirred Cell and a
10kDA cut-off nitrocellulose membrane for each sample.

    5. Protein purification
    The cells were lysed by using 5ml of B-PER complete lysis buffer (ThermoFischer
Scientific - Tris based) per 1g of cells, supplemented with protease inhibitor tablets. The
lysate was separated from lysed cells by centrifuging at 14.000g, 4°C for 20 minutes and
subsequently filtered through 0.22µm filters. The purification of his-tagged proteins from the
lysate and production media was performed by affinity chromatography using HisTrapTM 5ml
HP columns in ÄKTA Pure 25M (Cytiva). Each fraction (intracellular or secreted) had its own
dedicated column to avoid contaminations with cytosol contaminants. Before purification,
samples were supplemented with imidazole to reach a final concentration of 20mM. The
buffer (A) for equilibration and washing of the affinity columns was composed of 40mM Tris-
HCl, 300mM NaCl, 20mM imidazole, pH 7.6, and Buffer (B) for elution was similar but with
500mM of imidazole instead of 20mM. After the elution, fractions were analyzed by SDS-
PAGE and western blotting and the ones containing the his-tagged proteins were pooled and
dialyzed to 20mM Tris-HCl, 150mM NaCl, pH 7.4 with the Slide-A-LyzerTM G3 Dialysis
Cassettes, 10K MWCO (ThermoFischer Scientific) at 4°C. Afterwards, the purified proteins
were aliquoted and stored at -80°C.

temperature.Finally, the membranes were imaged using the AI600 Imager with CCD

membrate, the total protein content of each lane was calculated from the provided Poncaeu
S staining images and a normalization factor was applied to the intensity levels obtained from
the Western blots. Then, the mean intensity of all the bands in the same membrane was
established and the ratio of sample normalized intensity to mean normalized intensity per
membrane was calculated, enabling comparison. This was repeated for every biological
replicate and the average ratio with standard error was used for the visuilation of the figures
in Biorender.

    7. ELISA
    ELISA against the his-tagged proteins was performed after 50x dilution of culture
    supernatant with the assay's dilution buffer. The kit and protocol that it used was the "His
    Tag ELISA Detection Kit" from Genescript.

    8. Proteomics

    Proteomic sample preparation
    The samples were processed using an SP3-workflow on a BioMek i7 liquid handling
workstations (Beckman Coulter). 10µg of each sample were normalized to a volume of 150
µl. For samples with lower amount than 10µg 150 µL were taken without dilution. All samples
were reduced in 10mM dithiothreitol at 56°C for 30min and alkylated in 20mM
chloroacetamide at room temperature for 30min. 25 µL Hydrophobic and hydrophilic Sera-
MagTM SpeedBeads (50µg/µL, Carboxylate-Modified, Cytiva) were added to the samples.
Proteins in the samples were precipitated on beads using acetonitrile (ACN, final
concentration 70%), washed twice with 70% ethanol, once with 100% ACN and dried at room
temperature. The beads were resuspended in digestion buffer (10mM CaCl2, 10% ACN in 50
mM HEPES) and proteins were digested with trypsin/LysC (0.5 µg, mass spec grade,
Promega) for two hours at 37°C, followed by a second digestions step with trypsin (0.2 µg,
mass spec grade, Promega) overnight at 37°C. The supernatants were transferred to new
tubes, the beads were washed with 50 mM HEPES and the supernatants were combined.

                                                       76
Peptide concentrations were determined using PierceTM Quantitative Fluorometric Peptide
Assay (Thermo Scientific). The samples were purified using PierceTM Peptide Desalting Spin
Columns (Thermo Scientific), according to the manufacturer´s instructions, and evaporated
to dryness in a SpeedVac concentrator.

    LC-MS analysis
    Peptides were dissolved in 3% ACN, 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid and 0.015% dodecyl--D-
maltoside and injected on an Easy-nLC-1200 chromatography system coupled to an Orbitrap
Fusion Lumos Tribrid Mass Spectrometer (both Thermo Scientific). The liquid
chromatography system was equipped with an Acclaim Pepmap 100 C18 trap column
(Thermo Scientific) and an in-house packed Reprosil-Pur C18 analytical column (75 m × 350
mm, particle size 3 m, Dr Maisch). The liquid chromatography flow was set to 300 nL/min,
peptides were eluted with a gradient of 5% to 45% solvent B in solvent A over 48 min, then
to 100% B over 2 min, and then kept at 100% B for 15 min. Solvent A was 0.2% formic acid
in water, solvent B was 80% ACN and 0.2% formic acid in water.
    The precursor ion mass spectra (MS1) were acquired at a resolution of 120 000 and an
m/z range of 375-1500. Using a cycle time of 2 seconds the most abundant precursors with
charges 2-7 were isolated with an m/z window of 1 and fragmented by higher-energy
collisional dissociation (HCD) set to 30%. Fragment spectra (MS2) were recorded in the
orbitrap at a resolution of 30 000 and dynamic exclusion was set to 10 sec.

    Data Analysis
    Data analysis was performed using Proteome Discoverer (ver 3.0, Thermo Fisher
Scientific) The data was matched against bacillus subtilis swissprot database (4191 entries,
April 2025) containing the submitted sequences of bovine collagen 20 kDa. Sequest HT was
used as search engine with a precursor tolerance of 10 ppm and a fragment ion tolerance of
0.02 Da. Tryptic peptides were accepted with 2 missed cleavages. Methionine and proline
oxidation were set as variable modifications and cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as
fixed modification. IMP-ptmRS node was used for site localization of post translational
modifications. Minora Feature Detector node was used for MS1 quantification.

    9. Circular dichroism analysis
    Native bovine collagen type I (C9791, Sigma-Aldrich), lumpfish and starfish (Mehdi
Abdollahi's group, Chalmers university of Technology) were dissolved in 0.2M acetic acid and

                                                       77
diluted at a concentration of 1mg/ml, 0.2mg/mL and 0.1mg/ml and maintained overnight at
4°C for triple-helix formation. The absorption of the different concentrations was scanned
from 190nm to 280nm with Chirascan CD spectrometer (Applied Photophysics) at 4°C, with
0.2M acetic acid as the baseline. The recombinant collagen samples and bovine collagen were
diluted at 0.2mg/ml in TBS and stored at 4°C overnight. The absorption at 190-280nm was
scanned by Chirascan at 4°C, with TBS as a baseline. The measurements were performed with
a thermostated quartz cell of 1mm length, at a wavelength increment of 1nm and the results
were derived from the mean of three consecutive scans. For the recombinant collagen, an
additional purification batch was measured for reproducibility purposes. The native and
recombinant collagens were subjected in thermal denaturation analysis by increasing the
temperature from 4°C to 60°C at a rate of 1°C per min. Throughout the heating process, the
circular dichroism (CD) signal intensity (mdeg) was measured at 221.5nm every minute in
triplicates. The temperature where 50% of the triple-helix is denatured (Td) was estimated
with the application of the Boltzmann sigmoidal equation and the extrapolation of V50.
